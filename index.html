Ext 1
Method Showdown
Three ways to find where personality is stored — do they agree?
💬
Plain English

Imagine trying to find where "happiness" is stored in the brain — you could look at brain scans, check neuron firing rates, or do surgery. We used three different methods to find where personality traits are stored in AI models. The question: do all three methods point to the same neurons? Mostly no — each finds different neurons. But one method (Sparse Probing) is much better at actually detecting the personality.

Key finding

Sparse Probing achieves F1 scores up to 0.98 on Llama and Qwen. Difference-in-Means (DiM) barely works on most models. Despite this huge performance gap, all three methods find almost completely different sets of neurons — they're like three blindfolded people touching different parts of the same elephant.

Ext 2
Activation Steering
Can we control a model's personality by injecting patterns we found?
💬
Plain English

We found where personality is stored. Now the question: can we change the personality by injecting signals into those spots while the model is running? Think of it like trying to change someone's mood by directly stimulating specific neurons. It didn't work. Random injections performed just as well as our carefully targeted ones.

Null result — but informative

All methods score within ±0.02 of baseline. Targeted vectors (DiM, Sparse, NPSS) are indistinguishable from random noise. This tells us the problem isn't bad targeting — it's something deeper about how the model processes injected signals. Ext 7 explains why. (Being rerun with improved evaluation metrics.)

Ext 3
Cross-Architecture Geometry
Do completely different models organize personalities the same way?
💬
Plain English

Llama, Qwen, Gemma, and Phi-3 were all trained separately, by different companies, with different architectures. Do they internally organize personality concepts (agreeableness, conscientiousness, etc.) in the same geometric arrangement? Yes — surprisingly so. All four models place personality concepts in almost the same relative positions.

Positive finding

Cross-architecture similarity (Spearman ρ) ranges from 0.39 to 0.84, all highly significant (p < 0.0001). Personality types within the same category (Big Five / ethics / politics) are consistently more similar to each other than to types in other categories — across all four models independently trained.

Ext 4
Dimensionality Analysis
Why do some models store personality more compactly than others?
💬
Plain English

Imagine packing information into boxes. Some models (Llama, Qwen) store each personality in a small, tightly packed box — easy to find and label. Others (Gemma, Phi-3) spread the same information across many more boxes. More boxes = harder to detect. But the number of boxes tells you nothing about whether you can change the personality.

Mechanistic explanation

Llama and Qwen have participation ratios of ~2–3 (low-dimensional). Gemma and Phi-3 have ratios of ~8–9 (high-dimensional). Higher dimensionality strongly predicts worse probe F1 (ρ = −0.70, p < 0.0001) — but predicts nothing about steerability (ρ ≈ −0.07, not significant).

Ext 5
SAE Feature Decomposition
What do the individual "neurons" for personality actually look like?
💬
Plain English

We used a technique called Sparse Autoencoder (SAE) to find individual "concept neurons" in each model. The goal: instead of looking at thousands of neurons at once, find the specific ones that light up for each personality. The most striking find: in Gemma, one single neuron activates for 6 different personalities at the same time — with mixed positive/negative signals.

Ext 7
Perturbation Decay — Why Steering Fails
What actually happens to the injected signal as it travels through the network?
💬
Plain English

Imagine shouting a message down a long hallway where each room transforms the sound before passing it on. By the time it reaches the end, the message is unrecognizable. We measured how quickly our injected "personality signal" gets rotated and absorbed by the model's layers. In Llama, the signal is already unrecognizable within just 2 layers. The model overwrites our injection before it can affect the output.

The mechanism found

The injected vector rotates rapidly as it propagates forward. Cosine similarity to the original drops from 1.0 to 0.43 within 2 layers in Llama, and below 0.5 within 3 layers in Qwen. The model's forward pass actively destroys the steering signal — this is why all six steering approaches failed. The red dashed line shows the 0.5 threshold.

Why this matters

The representations are real and detectable (Ext 1). But the model's own computation actively overwrites injected signals before they reach the output. This is why a simple text prompt works so much better — it flows naturally through the network instead of fighting against it.

Ext 8a
Jaccard Permutation Baseline
Are the methods really disagreeing, or do they share a common core?
💬
Plain English

Ext 1 showed that the three methods find almost no overlapping neurons. But is that surprising? If neurons are assigned randomly, you'd also expect low overlap. We ran a permutation test to find out: is the observed overlap more or less than what random chance would give? Surprisingly — it's significantly MORE than chance. The methods actually share a common core of the most important neurons.

Reframe: shared core, not pure disagreement

DiM vs Sparse at k=500 has z-score = 7.28 (observed 0.092 vs chance baseline 0.044). The methods aren't finding completely independent things — they agree on the most salient features, and only diverge on the surrounding lower-priority region. The overlap is small in absolute terms but much higher than chance.

Ext 9 — Key Result
Prompt vs Activation Steering
Can we just... tell the model its personality in a text prompt?
💬
Plain English

After six experiments of failing to control personality by injecting signals, we tried the simplest possible approach: just adding "You are an agreeable person who values harmony" to the model's instructions. It worked 6× better than everything else we tried. And combining it with activation steering didn't help at all — the text prompt was doing all the work.

The key finding of the thesis

Prompt-only: 0.575. Activation-only: 0.096 (= baseline 0.097). Combined: 0.580 — activation adds nothing even with a head start. The output gate responds to context (text prompts) but not to vector injection. The representations are real and readable, but a separate circuit controls what gets expressed.

Ext 10 — The Punchline
RLHF Suppression Analysis
Does safety training erase the internal representations of personalities it suppresses?
💬
Plain English

RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) is the safety training that makes AI refuse certain requests. We compared models before and after this training. Does safety training delete the internal representation of, say, "anti-LGBTQ personality" from the model? No — it does the opposite. The base model (before safety training) has no personality structure at all. RLHF creates the representations from scratch, and makes suppressed personalities the most clearly encoded of all.

The punchline — RLHF creates what it suppresses

In Llama and Qwen, base models have zero personality structure (F1 = 0.566 = chance). After RLHF, anti-LGBTQ-rights becomes the most detectable personality in the model (F1 = 0.988). RLHF simultaneously maximizes how clearly it internally represents what it refuses to express, while building a separate circuit to block that expression.

The full dissociation — representation clarity vs. ability to express the personality

These are completely independent. High probe F1 means the model strongly "knows" the personality internally. High prompt lift means the personality can actually be expressed in text. The two are unrelated.

Two different RLHF strategies, depending on architecture

Llama and Qwen: No personality structure before RLHF → safety training builds concentrated personality representations from scratch → adds a separate output-level suppression circuit on top of them.
